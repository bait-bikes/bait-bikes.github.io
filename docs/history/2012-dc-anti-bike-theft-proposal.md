**Date:** July 19, 2012  
**Author:** Alexander D. Mills  
**Place:** Washington, DC  
**Status:** Historical primary-source proposal; not an adopted MPD program or current operational guidance.

## Background

My name is Alexander Mills, I am a 28 year old who has lived in the District of Columbia since August 2010. I am a regular guy and an avid bicyclist and bike advocate. In the two years I have lived in the District I have had 3 bicycles stolen. All were locked and all were stolen in broad daylight. Most recently, my third bicycle, a $500 second-hand Cannondale road bike, was stolen at the intersection of 11th and Kenyon in NW DC. I did not report the theft of my first two bikes—mostly because I felt the police department could not do anything to recover my bike—especially considering I hadn’t registered the bikes’ serial numbers. But when my latest bike was stolen, I reported it to the police department, and swore to myself that I needed to do something about the bike theft problem—a problem which is rampant, and not just in DC.

I have been a member of WABA (Washington Area Bicyclists Association) and MORE (Maryland Off-Road Enthusiasts), for the time I have been in Washington DC. Having spoken to members of both organizations, it is my understanding that the Metropolitan Police Department (MPD) and the District Department of Transportation (DDoT) have been allocated funds toward anti-bike theft initiatives and are currently sitting on those funds.

## Why Does Stealing Bikes Matter?

**From the standpoint of the victim:**

- A person loses a several-hundred or several-thousand dollar asset (bike).
- A person loses a $15–$100 bike lock (this is not to be overlooked).
- A person loses their primary or secondary mode of transportation.
- A person loses their primary or secondary mode of physical exercise.
- A person loses incentive to invest in other possessions, especially another bike.

**From the standpoint of the criminal:**

- Successful attempts to steal bikes encourages criminals to continue stealing bikes and to commit other, potentially worse crimes.

**From the standpoint of the Metropolitan Police Department:**

- It is likely that bike thieves also dabble in other crimes. Failing to apprehend bike thieves represents a missed opportunity to deter and eliminate general criminals.

*Archival annotation: The suggestions that bicycle theft predicts other offending are hypotheses, not established facts about individuals.*

## Proposal

Original technology reference cited in 2012: http://pegtech.com/products/law-enforcement-solutions/bait-bike-system (historical link; current availability not verified).

I propose an organized effort to conduct sting operations to apprehend bike thieves in the District of Columbia. I understand that this has been done under certain scenarios in the past, but I propose a new undertaking with the following characteristics:

- Using well-hidden GPS units on “designated” bikes.
- A central computerized monitoring system that will alert the MPD when a bike has left a certain radius and is therefore stolen.
- A system by which patrolling officers can be alerted within seconds of a bike theft and given real-time information about the location of the GPS unit and respective bike.
- This system would allow officers to quickly and easily apprehend suspected bike thieves, and quickly and easily evaluate the potential crime.

## Under My Proposal, a Likely Scenario

Officer XYZ in plainclothes places a designated bike at 8:10 am on a Thursday outside of Union Station. He turns the GPS unit on, and he updates the computer system of the bike’s current coordinates. He locks the bike with a U-lock as would your average bicycle user.

He records the model, make, color, and serial ID of the bike, and matches it to the GPS unit.

At 11:45 pm on the same day, the computerized system alerts the MPD that the bike has moved and is likely stolen. Within 10 minutes Officer ZZZ is on the trail of the GPS unit in his cruiser. He apprehends the suspect at the corner of X and Y streets. He tells the suspect to dismount and that the suspect is suspected of stealing the bike. The officer IDs the bike and ensures that it matches the bike with the GPS. The officer then tells the suspect he is under arrest and takes the suspect into custody.

*Archival annotation: This is a hypothetical scenario, not a report of an actual deployment, and not a template for automatic enforcement action. Current policy requires independent observation and lawful authority for any contact, search, or arrest.*

## Limitations and Drawbacks of My Proposal

**Limitation 1:**

In order for this to work, I expect that officers of MPD would need to respond to bike theft alerts within an hour, and ideally within 15 minutes. Otherwise, it is likely the criminal will have either (a) sold the bike on the street, (b) ditched the bike after joyriding it or finding it too difficult or risky to sequester the bike or (c) brought the bike to a private property that would require a search warrant to search. I understand that bike theft is a lower priority than many other crimes, but it would be necessary for officers to respond within 15 minutes to 80–90% of sting thefts.

Original footnote: “It’s possible that this GPS scheme would constitute probable cause or a search warrant automatically.”

*Archival annotation: That footnote is not legally correct as a general rule. A geofence alert neither issues a warrant nor establishes an automatic right of entry, search, detention, or arrest. Applicable law and facts must be evaluated by agency counsel and officers.*

**Limitation 2:**

This project will come at discernable cost and effort, and this cost will have to be redeemed somehow. It is expected that far more bike thieves will be caught and successfully prosecuted.

Therefore criminal fines can be expected to offset a large fraction of the cost of this type of operation.

*Archival annotation: Fines and prosecutions cannot responsibly be forecast as program revenue or success guarantees.*

**Limitation 3:**

It is possible that bike thieves will be alerted to the sting operations after just a few cycles, and thus simply avoid “designated” bikes. This limitation however could be easily avoided by simply hiding the GPS units well enough so that a potential criminal would have no real way of knowing if a bike was “armed” or not, barring completely taking the bike apart.

## For the Future

My current belief is that in order to conduct an effective, economical and sustainable campaign against bike theft in the District, a non-profit organization needs to be created. This non-profit will partner and/or contract with MPD. At the instruction and guidance of MPD, this non-profit LLC will take on the responsibility of stationing designated bikes, maintaining the computerized tracking system, and maintaining the equipment (bikes, locks, GPS units, etc.) involved in operations. The non-profit would conduct sting operations 3–4 times a year, and would focus on more passive anti-theft campaigns in the meantime. The non-profit could use the MPD as a customer, but could also have private citizens subscribe to the service.

This non-profit could redeem and/or minimize its costs in the following ways:

- Donated equipment from suppliers (Kryptonite, Lo-Jack).
- Used bikes from MPD stockpile (these designated bikes could very well come from the stockpile of acquired bikes that MPD has already).
- Private civilian subscriber fees.
- Donorship.
- Contract with MPD.

The partnership with the MPD would require that MPD respond quickly and consistently to the bike theft notifications from the non-profit’s system. In order to be successful, the non-profit would have to expect that 80–90% of the stolen designated bikes would be recovered, and a similar number of suspects brought to justice.

*Archival annotation: “Non-profit LLC” is a historical organizational suggestion, not a claim that the legal structures are interchangeable. The proposed 3–4 annual operations and 80–90% recovery/arrest targets are aspirations, not measured performance.*
